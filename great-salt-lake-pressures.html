Where the water goes

Most of the water used in the lake's basin goes to farms

Rivers carry snowmelt toward the lake. Along the way, people use some of it up. This is called depletion: water that is consumed and does not flow back. The pins on the map above are individual projects; the biggest uses, farms and lawns, are spread across thousands of fields and yards and cannot be shown as a single pin.

Agriculture is the largest use by far (65%). Within city use, outdoor watering at homes is the largest part: 96.9% of residential depletion, added up across hundreds of thousands of homes. Watering home lawns and landscaping alone used an estimated 408,500 acre-feet in 2024, about a quarter as much as agriculture in the basin. Golf courses are counted separately, under business use (about 23,600 acre-feet in 2022).
Totals vs. one user: why both matter

Added together, homes use more water than today's data centers. But that total is spread across hundreds of thousands of households. A single large user can equal hundreds or thousands of homes, and some of the largest users have no reporting or cut requirement.

No public number for most business water. Business and industry are 8% of statewide diversions, about 408,000 acre-feet a year. Only golf courses (voluntary reports) and seven named data centers have public figures, together about 6% of that. Offices, stores, churches, schools, manufacturing, and other buildings have no public user-by-user accounting.
Who faced required cuts. In 2025, required cuts applied to mineral companies. Cuts for agriculture were voluntary and paid through incentives. The Strike Team says: "all sectors contribute to depletions in the basin and no single sector can bear the full responsibility for restoring the lake."

The June 2026 state-commissioned report gives agriculture as about 63% and municipal use as about 11% of consumptive use. That report measures differently from the water budget shown above; the two sets of figures are not directly comparable.

Leasing has grown about nine times since 2021. It is still about a fifth of the yearly target.
Who makes these deals. Legislators do not lease the water themselves. They passed the laws that allow it and set aside money. The deals are made between:
  • Water-right holders (farmers, irrigation companies, water districts, and cities), who lease or donate water to the lake and keep their underlying rights.
  • The Great Salt Lake Watershed Enhancement Trust, run by the National Audubon Society and The Nature Conservancy with $40 million from the Legislature (H.B. 410, 2022). It reports 59,000 to 72,485 acre-feet a year.
  • The state: the Office of the Great Salt Lake Commissioner and the Division of Forestry, Fire and State Lands, including a $50 million Water Delivery Program run with the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (2025). The Trust Council reviews the commissioner's water purchases and leases.
The Strike Team's 163,468 acre-feet is a total. The records reviewed do not break it down by who arranged each deal.

Drinking water or secondary water?

  • The six figures come from city water providers' records, meaning water bought through city systems. The reporting reviewed does not say, facility by facility, whether it is treated drinking (culinary) water or secondary water.
  • NSA, Bluffdale: an archived Salt Lake Tribune report says Bluffdale contracted, effective Sept. 30, 2011, to sell the NSA water at $2.05 per 1,000 gallons, below the city's $3.25 commercial rate, and describes Bluffdale as a culinary-water supplier.
  • Novva's CEO, Wes Swenson, said in 2022 that other data centers are "using culinary water, not irrigation [water], so it's been treated by the city." That is his statement, not a records finding.
  • Meta buys its water through Eagle Mountain. Joule, in Millard County, uses its own groundwater rights.
  • By comparison, many Wasatch Front homes use drinking water indoors and untreated secondary water for lawns. The 2030 meter requirement applies to that secondary water.

New water approved during the freeze

Gov. Cox closed the lake basin to most new water rights in November 2022. In the two years after, about 300 applications asked for more than 230,000 acre-feet. The state approved about 2,517 acre-feet of new rights, mostly for irrigation and stock water.

The largest approval found was Keller Cattle Corp. (associated with House Speaker Mike Schultz): 556.7 acre-feet a year near Promontory, approved July 2024. That is about 22% of all new water approved in the basin in those two years (Hive calculation: 556.7 ÷ 2,517). A nearby applicant, Mango-Spiral Jetty LLC, was approved for 240 acre-feet of a larger request and has challenged the decision in court (see map pins).

The closure has exceptions: uses that return the water (such as hydropower), applications with a plan to offset water used, and small amounts. Source: Salt Lake Tribune / Great Salt Lake Collaborative, Feb. 10, 2025 (Division of Water Rights data through Oct. 29, 2024); Governor's Proclamation 2022-01.

Words on this map

Acre-footTap to flip ↻
The water needed to cover one acre one foot deep, about 326,000 gallons. Water rights and lake use are measured this way.
Water rightTap to flip ↻
A legal right to use a set amount of water for a set purpose. Utah's State Engineer approves new rights and changes to old ones.
Change applicationTap to flip ↻
A request to change where, how, or for what an existing water right is used. Anyone affected can protest it.
ProtestTap to flip ↻
A formal objection filed with the State Engineer while an application is open for comment.
Proof of beneficial useTap to flip ↻
The deadline to show the water has actually been put to use. A right that is never used can lapse.
UPDES permitTap to flip ↻
Utah's permit for discharging wastewater or other pollutants into state waters, issued by the Division of Water Quality.
Antidegradation reviewTap to flip ↻
A Water Quality review of whether a new discharge would lower the quality of water that is currently cleaner than required.
Reverse osmosis brineTap to flip ↻
The salty leftover water produced when reverse osmosis filters make fresh water.
North Arm and South ArmTap to flip ↻
A railroad causeway splits the lake. The North Arm (Gunnison Bay) is much saltier; the South Arm (Gilbert Bay) gets most of the river water.
CurtailmentTap to flip ↻
A required cut in water use. Mineral companies' use is cut when the lake falls below a set level.
Severance taxTap to flip ↻
A tax on minerals taken from the land or the lake. Utah raised the rate for lake minerals in 2024.
Project areaTap to flip ↻
The boundary a state authority such as the Inland Port or MIDA draws. Its tax and development rules apply only inside that line.
